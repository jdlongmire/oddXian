# Ancient Verification Practices and Pauline Public Contestability v0.1

WP-OX-RES-0001 | 2026-10-08

## Research question

How would an ancient audience assess a claim circulated while purported witnesses were alive, and how does 1 Corinthians 15:6 compare with ancient historiographical standards?

## Ancient methodological controls

**Polybius, Histories 12.27:** Polybius criticizes reliance on books alone and explicitly values the interrogation of living witnesses, firsthand observation, and difficult personal inquiry. This establishes that checking living witnesses was a recognized historiographical ideal, not a modern invention. It does not prove that Corinthians applied that ideal to Paul's letter. Primary text: https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0234%3Abook%3D12%3Achapter%3D27

**Josephus, Against Apion 1:** Josephus defends his account of the Jewish War by citing personal participation, firsthand observation, consultation with knowledgeable persons, and circulation of his work to commanders and other informed contemporaries who could object. This is a striking *structural comparator* for the rhetoric of accessible contemporaries and public scrutiny, although it concerns ordinary historical events and is an author's self-defense rather than external proof that every claim was checked. Primary text: https://penelope.uchicago.edu/josephus/apion-1.html

**Josephus, Against Apion 1, on documentary records:** Josephus also argues that preserved public records constrain fabrication. This demonstrates an ancient understanding of record-based falsifiability; it does not establish that every resurrection appearance had such documentation.

## Pauline case

- **1 Cor 15:1–3:** Paul appeals to a message preached to and received by an identifiable congregation.
- **1 Cor 15:5–7:** The letter records named recipients and a claim that more than five hundred saw Jesus at one time, most still alive.
- **1 Cor 15:8 and 9:1:** Paul personally claims an encounter; these are two attestations from the same author, not independent eyewitnesses.
- **1 Cor 15:12:** The letter itself documents disagreement about resurrection of the dead. The appearance-to-500 report has no identified surviving direct rebuttal; this does not mean it was undisputed.
- **Gal 1:18–19:** Paul reports meeting Cephas and James. Access is plausible; the content of any verification conversation is not preserved.

## Conditional challenge analysis

For the 500 report to have been **effectively falsifiable**, it matters whether members of the group were identifiable and geographically reachable; whether critics knew the precise claim; whether social, political and travel costs permitted inquiry; and whether negative results would have been written and preserved. Paul's living-witness assertion increases potential exposure to checking but leaves these conditions unresolved.

A report of a large group is more specific than an isolated anonymous apparition story. But the surviving datum is one authored claim about a group, not 500 separate depositions. A naturalistic composite hypothesis must explain the genesis and retention of that group report; a resurrection hypothesis must explain why the extant record lacks independent group testimony without inventing convenient preservation assumptions. Neither side may count mere possibility as evidence.

## New distinction: acceptance versus verification

Reception of a proclamation is an observable social fact reported by Paul; acceptance of its truth by congregants is a reasonable interpretation of his wording but need not be unanimous; independent corroboration of the underlying event is a different proposition. Ancient historiographical ideals do not establish that Paul or his audience conducted formal inquiry.

## Provisional conclusion

The 500 claim has a meaningful **historical-contestability feature**: it was recorded relatively early, circulated to an identifiable audience, and explicitly invoked the continued life of purported group recipients. Polybius and Josephus show that checking living persons and exposure to knowledgeable contemporaries were intelligible ancient evidentiary practices. The stronger conclusion, that Paul's claim was in fact checked and went unchallenged, is not established.
