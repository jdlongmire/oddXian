# Public Circulation, Reception, and Contestability Audit v0.1

WP-OX-RES-0001 | 2026-10-08

## Core thesis

Historical comparison must evaluate not only similarity of miracle motifs but the documented *social and evidentiary situation* of a claim: early recording, identified recipients, circulation to an identifiable audience, reception, witness accessibility, and surviving challenge. This is a proposed discriminator, not an already established demonstration of uniqueness.

## Primary-text anchors

**1 Corinthians 15:1–8.** Paul reminds the Corinthians of the gospel he preached, which they 'received' and in which they stand (vv. 1–2), says he transmitted what he received (v. 3), and reports an appearance to more than five hundred 'at one time', most of whom 'are still alive', though some have died (v. 6). The extant evidence is Paul's *single surviving report of a group appearance*, not 500 separately preserved attestations. Paul's statement about living witnesses is an explicit contemporaneity claim; it does not establish that Corinthians contacted those witnesses or that their identities and locations were supplied.

**1 Corinthians 15:12–19.** Paul addresses people within the Christian community saying there is no resurrection of the dead. This is **documented dispute over resurrection doctrine**, so claims that the resurrection was accepted 'without dispute' require qualification. It does not show that these people specifically denied the report about the 500.

**Galatians 1:18–19; 2:1–10.** Paul claims contact with Cephas, James and other leaders. This supports a potential channel for checking reports, not proof that a specific verification occurred.

**1 Corinthians 9:1.** Paul invokes having seen Jesus in defense of his apostolic status. The statement strengthens the case that the claim was integral to his public authority, but is still the same author's testimony.

## New parity dimensions

| Dimension | What is directly attested? | What remains an inference? |
|---|---|---|
| Early recording | Paul's letter was written within the first generation after Jesus' death | Exact date of each inherited appearance item |
| Claim circulation | Paul states he preached and transmitted the resurrection message to Corinthians | Breadth of circulation outside documented communities |
| Community reception | Paul says Corinthians received and stood in the preached gospel | Uniform acceptance, acceptance by opponents, or historical verification |
| Large-group appearance | Paul reports 'more than five hundred at one time' | 500 independent witnesses, identities, locations, sensory conditions |
| Living-witness assertion | Paul says most were still alive when he wrote | Actual accessibility, interviews, or confirmation by named persons |
| Contestability | Publicly communicated claims could in principle be challenged | Demonstrated opportunities, ability to travel, and likelihood of preservation |
| Surviving rebuttal | No identified extant contemporary text specifically rebuts the 500 report | That nobody disputed the claim or that silence validates it |
| Internal doctrinal dispute | 1 Cor 15:12 directly records denial of resurrection of the dead by some | That the group appearance itself was rejected or accepted by those individuals |

## Comparative ancient cases

**Romulus:** Plutarch, *Romulus* 27–28, records Proculus's alleged sworn testimony but does so centuries after the traditional founding-era event. The source does not preserve a contemporary public letter to an identifiable community claiming most of a large group of eyewitnesses remained alive.

**Aristeas:** Herodotus, *Histories* 4.14–15, records a contested death and reports of later sightings. Crucially, it contains **internal challenge and competing testimony** about Aristeas's death, which must not be overlooked when assessing public contestation. Herodotus reports townspeople's traditions rather than personally claiming an encounter.

**Apollonius:** Philostratus, *Life of Apollonius* 8.29–31, describes disputed death traditions and a later dream appearance. The surviving literary account is later and does not provide a Pauline-style contemporaneous group-witness accessibility claim.

**Cleomedes:** Pausanias, *Description of Greece* 6.9, recounts disappearance and heroic elevation; it does not document a postmortem group encounter followed by a surviving contemporary letter to a known congregation.

**Scope limitation:** This sample does not prove that no other ancient case satisfies the proposed conjunction. Search criteria and comparators must be expanded before an exhaustive claim.

## Silence and expected-record analysis

A missing rebuttal has evidentiary force only to the extent that, under the counter-hypothesis, a rebuttal would likely have been (1) formulated, (2) transmitted to an accessible audience, (3) recorded in a medium likely to survive, and (4) preserved and identified by modern researchers. None of these conditional probabilities is established for the 500 report. Therefore 'uncontested' is **not** an admissible description; 'no surviving specific rebuttal identified' is.

The letter itself records internal dispute concerning resurrection (1 Cor 15:12), so community reception and doctrinal unanimity must not be conflated.

## Hypothesis discrimination

**Bodily resurrection (R):** An actual group encounter could explain a contemporaneous report and Paul's claim that witnesses remained alive. It does not automatically predict that a letter would name all participants or that rebuttals would survive.

**Early experiences plus transmission (C):** Early sincere experiences and communal tradition can explain circulation and reception. A large simultaneous appearance report adds a specific burden: C must account for how the group claim originated, spread, and was retained by Paul. However, C need not posit 500 separate hallucinations unless the group's independent experiences are first established.

**Fabrication (F):** Public circulation with purportedly living witnesses potentially increases exposure to contradiction, but this only discriminates if witness accessibility and incentives to challenge can be demonstrated.

## Research tasks

1. Establish what 'received' (παρελάβετε) and 'delivered' (παρέδωκα) demonstrate about teaching and reception in 1 Cor 15:1–3, using comparative Pauline usage.
2. Investigate the extent to which 1 Cor 15:6 could function as an invitation to verification versus a rhetorical appeal to surviving contemporaries.
3. Catalogue early Christian and non-Christian counterclaims concerning resurrection and theft, with date, author, original source, and transmission dependence; do not project later polemics backward uncritically.
4. Search for ancient claims that combine large named groups, living-witness assertions, and circulation in near-contemporary correspondence.
5. Add explicit 'public contestability' and 'documented reception' columns to the broader historical parity matrix.
6. Reassess likelihoods only after documenting actual channels of verification and counterclaim survival.

## Provisional conclusion

The Pauline letter provides a historically important conjunction: early written circulation to an identifiable community, a claim of received teaching, a specific simultaneous group-appearance report, and an assertion that most group recipients remained alive. These are meaningful **features of the surviving claim** that deserve parity testing. They do not transform one report into 500 depositions, establish universal acceptance, or show that the claim was historically undisputed.
