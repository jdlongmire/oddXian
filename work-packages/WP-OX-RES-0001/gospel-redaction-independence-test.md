# Gospel Redaction and Independence: Controlled Pericope Test v0.1

WP-OX-RES-0001 | 2026-10-08

## Scope

Test whether the distinctive resurrection narratives are better explained by independent historical memory, literary redaction, or both. No inference may rest on counting four canonical Gospels as four independent depositions. Do not weight scholars by their religious or skeptical affiliations.

## Pericope-level observations

| Motif | Mark 16:1–8 | Matthew 28 | Luke 24 | John 20–21 | Dependency-sensitive interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Women at tomb | Mary Magdalene, Mary mother of James, Salome | Mary Magdalene, other Mary | Mary Magdalene, Joanna, Mary mother of James, others | Mary Magdalene foregrounded; 'we' in 20:2 | Stable women-at-tomb motif across narratives; names and number vary. Stability does not establish independent eyewitness access. |
| Visit purpose | Spices to anoint | To see tomb | Spices carried | Purpose not stated in 20:1 | Matthew's omission is plausibly related to its guards narrative; redaction is an explanatory candidate, not demonstrated invention. |
| Messenger | Young man inside | Angel, with guards | Two dazzling men | Two angels appear later to Mary | Core interpretive motif with divergent narrative details. |
| Tomb report | Fear/silence at 16:8 | Fear and joy; departure to tell | Women tell apostles; dismissed | Mary tells Peter and beloved disciple body removed | Literary and theological shaping is evident, but the existence of shaping does not determine historicity. |
| Appearance to Mary/women | None in recoverable 16:1–8 | Jesus meets women | None in opening tomb episode | Jesus meets Mary | Possible distinct traditions, shared tradition, or literary development. |
| Male tomb inspection | None | None | Peter runs and sees cloths, 24:12 | Peter and beloved disciple inspect, 20:3–10 | Shared details may imply common tradition or literary relationship; not two independently preserved depositions. |
| Geography | Galilee promised | Galilee commission, also women meet Jesus | Jerusalem-centered appearances | Jerusalem in ch. 20; Galilee in ch. 21 | Distinct geography cannot be ignored or automatically harmonized. |
| Bodily demonstration | No narrated appearance | Women grasp feet | Jesus offers hands/feet, eats | Wounds and Thomas episode | Strong textual evidence of bodily claims; chronology and independence of details remain open. |

## Competing explanatory models

**I, partially independent historical memory:** Some distinctive episodes derive from separate firsthand or near-firsthand transmission. Predicts stable core details with ordinary variation; requires a credible provenance route, not simply nonidentical wording.

**D, predominantly literary development:** Later writers adapt earlier accounts and shared community traditions to express theological and narrative interests. Predicts identifiable reuse, changes serving literary aims, and later detail expansion; does not entail that every underlying event is invented.

**H, hybrid:** Earlier remembered events or testimony are transmitted through both shared traditions and literary adaptation. Predicts both inherited motifs and editorial differences. Because H is flexible, it must specify which motifs have independent provenance and cannot claim all discrepancies as confirmation.

## Adversarial checks

- A repeated motif is not multiple independent attestations if later authors reuse earlier sources.
- A distinctive detail is not independent attestation unless its provenance is demonstrated.
- Editorial adaptation does not logically entail fabrication; historical writing can select and arrange real material.
- An apparent inconsistency is a datum requiring examination, not automatic disproof; a harmonization is a hypothesis requiring evidence, not automatic proof.
- Women's priority in all four narratives is a notable literary datum. Arguments from embarrassment or women's lower legal status must be historically contextualized and cannot substitute for source provenance.
- John's 'we do not know' (20:2) may preserve memory of companions, or reflect narration and source adaptation; it cannot by itself establish the number present.

## Preliminary conclusion

The textual evidence favors some literary adaptation, but cannot by itself choose between independent remembered events and development of shared traditions. The women-at-tomb motif is stable, while accompanying details vary; Matthew and Luke's use of Mark in much shared material must be modeled explicitly. The hybrid explanation is plausible but must not become an unfalsifiable default. Neither a strong independent-eyewitness claim nor an all-invented claim is justified by this comparison alone.
