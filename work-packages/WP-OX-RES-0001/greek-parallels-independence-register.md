# Greek Parallel and Proposition-Specific Independence Register v0.1

WP-OX-RES-0001 | 2026-10-08

## Method

Assess the earliest recoverable text of Mark 16:1–8 alongside Matthew 28:1–8, Luke 24:1–12 and John 20:1–18. Distinguish (a) shared wording, (b) shared motif, (c) literary dependence, (d) historically independent access, and (e) the truth of the narrated event. These are not equivalent. This first pass is a targeted lexical comparison, **not** a full Greek collation or computational similarity analysis.

## Targeted Greek readings

- Mark 16:1: `ἠγόρασαν ἀρώματα` (they bought spices), followed by `ἵνα ... ἀλείψωσιν αὐτόν` (purpose of anointing him).
- Luke 24:1: `φέρονσαι ἃ ἡτοίμασαν ἀρώματα` (bringing spices they had prepared). The shared noun `ἀρώματα` is meaningful motif/lexical overlap, not proof of direct copying on its own.
- Matthew 28:1: `θεωρῆσαι τὸν τάφον` (to see the tomb); the spices/anointing purpose is absent.
- John 20:1: Mary Magdalene visits early and sees the stone removed. John 20:2 has `οὐκ οἴδαμεν` ('we do not know'), which may imply companions or reflect inherited narrative speech; it cannot prove the number of women present.

## Independence register

**Ratings describe support for *separate historical information streams*, not event probability.**

| Proposition | Earliest relevant streams | Literary dependence risk | Separate historical stream confidence | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Women visit tomb | Mark; Matthew/Luke; John | High for Synoptic parallels; uncertain for John | **Unresolved** | Markan priority offers plausible dependence; John's distinct narration does not alone establish separate eyewitness provenance |
| Mary Magdalene associated with tomb discovery | Mark and later Gospels | High within Synoptics | **Unresolved** | Agreement is notable, but can propagate through common tradition |
| Stone removed / absence of body narrated | Mark; later Gospels | High for Synoptic core | **Unresolved** | Repeated textual report is not multiple independent inspections |
| Tomb messenger interprets absence as resurrection | Mark; Matthew/Luke; John | High/medium depending on pericope | **Unresolved** | Core may derive from shared proclamation; John's scene is structured differently |
| Peter's tomb inspection | Luke 24:12; John 20:3–10 | Literary or common-source relation disputed | **Unresolved** | Overlap could be shared source or direct relation; neither is a preserved deposition from Peter |
| Jesus appears to women | Matthew 28:9–10; John 20:11–18 | Shared tradition or independent development possible | **Unresolved** | Different details are not sufficient proof of independence |
| Jerusalem gathered-disciple appearances | Luke 24:36–49; John 20:19–29 | Shared tradition or dependence possible | **Unresolved** | Physicality and setting overlap; direct historical source routes unestablished |
| Galilee appearance tradition | Mark 16:7 (promise); Matthew 28:16–20; John 21 | Mark/Matthew connection clear at thematic level; John uncertain | **Unresolved** | Mark's promise is not a narrated appearance |
| Early named appearance list | 1 Cor 15:3–8 | Paul transmits received tradition | **Established distinct literary attestation from later Gospel texts; historical independence unresolved** | Earlier letter, but shared apostolic proclamation could underlie later reports |
| Paul's own claimed encounter | 1 Cor 15:8; Gal 1 | Same author, not two independent witnesses | **Strong for distinct firsthand Pauline claim** | Paul's direct self-report is separate from later Gospel narratives, though interpretation remains disputed |

**Do not turn unresolved into low probability of historicity.** Unresolved means provenance is insufficiently demonstrated.

## Scholarly stress tests

- Davila (2026) argues that Mark's resurrection announcement is integrated with his kingdom-of-God theology. This is evidence that Mark's presentation is theologically purposeful, not evidence that its underlying tradition is necessarily fabricated. Full article: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/new-testament-studies/article/does-mark-care-that-jesus-was-raised-from-the-dead-on-resurrecting-the-king-and-the-kingdom-mark-1618/38D02EE65DBAAD30E9ACC4D53509A635
- Meyer (2025) argues that differences in the women's roles may reflect redaction that suppresses earlier women's authority. This is a substantive counterweight to assuming all later changes are embellishment, but its proposed direction of development must itself be demonstrated. https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/09518207251367408
- Miller (2004) reads Mark's women's silence within the narrative's theological themes; Mark 16:8 should not be read as a literal claim that no resurrection message was ever transmitted. Publisher abstract: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/096673500401300106
- Cranfield (1952) already identified the tension between women's priority in the Gospel narratives and Peter's priority in Paul's appearance list. This is an old source-critical question, not a newly discovered contradiction. Publisher excerpt: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/scottish-journal-of-theology/article/abs/st-mark-16181/A97A67B9FC3E2913D295A4422CC43AF0

## Historical comparison implications

The early Pauline appearance list and the Gospel tomb tradition are different literary units with different emphases. Their convergence on resurrection proclamation is historically important. Their independent access to an objective bodily event is **not established** by the current record. Conversely, shared proclamation and editorial development do **not** establish a naturalistic causal explanation.
