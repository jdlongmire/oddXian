# WP-OX-RES-0001: The Resurrection as a Comparative Inductive Historical Argument

**Status:** Proposed research work package  
**Repository:** jdlongmire/oddxian  
**Author:** James D. (JD) Longmire  
**Date initiated:** 2026-10-08  
**Type:** Historical apologetics / epistemology / comparative inference

## Research question

When Jesus' resurrection is evaluated alongside accepted ancient historical events and comparable extraordinary-event claims using consistent evidentiary criteria, how strongly does the surviving evidence support the resurrection, and how much of the disagreement results from differing background assumptions?

## Thesis under investigation

The early resurrection proclamation may possess an exceptional combination of temporal proximity, identifiable witnesses, source diversity, reported appearances, conversion of an opponent, and movement-level consequences. Its distinctiveness and cumulative evidence warrant comparative inductive evaluation. Uniqueness, historicity, and supernatural causation must each be established separately; none is assumed at the outset.

## Objectives

1. **Minimal Facts baseline:** Define Habermas's narrow and expanded formulations, distinguishing facts accepted by scholars from disputed claims.
2. **Historical novelty:** Compare the resurrection narrative's specific features with ancient apotheosis, return-from-death, postmortem appearance, and extraordinary-event traditions.
3. **Inductive evidence:** Evaluate documentary proximity, testimony, transmission, named witnesses, corroboration, and source dependence. Do not count reported witnesses as independently extant testimonies.
4. **Historical evidentiary parity:** Compare the standards applied to the resurrection with those used for accepted ordinary and unusual events of antiquity.
5. **Bayesian analysis:** Model posterior odds and likelihood ratios with explicit alternative hypotheses, sensitivity ranges, and dependency adjustments. Avoid invented numerical probabilities.
6. **Rival explanations:** Evaluate resurrection, visionary experiences, legend development, fabrication, mistaken identification, and mixed explanations on identical criteria.
7. **Worldview assumptions:** Distinguish methodological constraints, metaphysical naturalism, theism, and Christian presuppositions. Examine how prior commitments affect admissibility and probability.
8. **Falsification and defeaters:** Identify evidence that would weaken the resurrection case or overturn a proposed uniqueness or parity claim.

## Comparative case set (initial, subject to revision)

- **Ordinary accepted events:** Assassination of Julius Caesar; execution of Socrates; selected campaigns of Alexander the Great.
- **Extraordinary historical claims:** Romulus's disappearance/apotheosis; Apollonius of Tyana traditions; other ancient postmortem appearances and restorations to life.
- **Target case:** Jesus' crucifixion, early resurrection proclamation, reported appearances, conversions of Paul and James, and empty-tomb traditions (separately scored).

## Evidence protocol

For each case record: (a) the exact proposition being evaluated; (b) primary source, date, genre, manuscript transmission, and accessibility; (c) whether access is direct or 'as cited in'; (d) independent attestation and dependence graph; (e) corroboration and contrary evidence; (f) accepted scholarly assessment with disagreement preserved; (g) event confidence versus explanatory confidence; (h) confidence label HIGH (verified primary), MEDIUM (secondary with pages), LOW (tertiary/paraphrase), or UNCERTAIN (insufficient basis). These labels describe source verification, not numerical probability of historicity. Never fabricate citations.

## Analytical safeguards

- Distinguish the fact that witnesses *claimed* an appearance from the occurrence of the claimed appearance.
- Do not infer that an event occurred solely because no parallel is known.
- Compare like propositions: event, testimony, mechanism, and downstream effects separately.
- Account for survival bias and asymmetric ancient documentation.
- Novelty can increase discriminative likelihood only when alternatives make the observed pattern less expected.
- Avoid double-counting correlated Gospel traditions or Paul's reported group witnesses.
- Ordinary historical acceptance and miracle adjudication may differ legitimately; test the reason rather than presupposing bias.
- Treat posterior conclusions as sensitive to priors and model specification.

## Workstreams and deliverables

**WS1: Source inventory.** Primary texts, dated witnesses, manuscript provenance, and critical scholarship bibliography.

**WS2: Comparative evidence ledger.** Reproducible event-by-event matrix with standardized criteria and explicit source dependencies.

**WS3: Hypothesis model.** Qualitative likelihood comparisons and optional bounded Bayesian sensitivity analysis, with assumptions disclosed.

**WS4: Adversarial review.** Strongest skeptical and Christian scholarly arguments, unresolved issues, potential counterexamples, and defeaters.

**WS5: Publication.** Scholarly white paper with Harvard author-year citations and an accessible oddXian essay.

## Acceptance criteria

- Every substantive historical claim traceable to a source; primary sources sought first.
- No invented scholarly consensus or numerical likelihoods.
- Distinct treatment of historical testimony, event occurrence, and supernatural explanation.
- At least one close extraordinary-event comparator and multiple ordinary-event benchmarks.
- Explicit source-dependency assessment and sensitivity to background assumptions.
- A clearly identified strongest objection and conditions under which the central thesis would fail.
