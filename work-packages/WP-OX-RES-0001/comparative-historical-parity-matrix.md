# Comparative Historical Evidentiary Parity Matrix v0.1

WP-OX-RES-0001 | 2026-10-08 | Provisional research increment

## Purpose and decision rule

Compare *matched propositions* rather than counting documents or comparing whole narratives. For each case distinguish (A) a source attests that a claim was made, (B) a person believed the claim, (C) the described event occurred, and (D) a proposed extraordinary cause is correct. Source-verification confidence must not be confused with event probability.

| Case and proposition | Nearest identifiable testimony | Independently extant testimony | Later narrative | Extraordinary causal claim | What present evidence warrants |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Caesar was assassinated, 44 BCE | Contemporary Cicero letters discussing the Ides and conspirators; Decimus Brutus letter describing immediate aftermath | Multiple contemporaneous political correspondence streams, with overlapping social networks | Plutarch *Caesar*; other later historians | No | Strong support for assassination; exact circumstances need passage-level comparison |
| Jesus was executed under Pilate | Early Christian texts including Pauline tradition | Tacitus later records execution, though his information pathway remains uncertain | Gospel passion narratives | No | Strong historical grounding of execution |
| Paul claimed an appearance of risen Jesus | Paul's own 1 Cor 15:8 and Gal 1:11–16 | No independent extant deposition of the appearance itself | Acts narratives, whose dependence and genre require separate analysis | Yes | Direct evidence that Paul made the claim, not independent demonstration of its objective cause |
| Peter, James, Twelve, 500 received appearances | Paul's report of a received tradition, 1 Cor 15:3–7 | No surviving firsthand statement from each named/listed person in this ledger | Later Gospel appearance narratives overlap some recipients | Yes | Strong evidence of an early proclamation; do not equate enumerated recipients with extant independent witnesses |
| Jesus' tomb was empty | Mark 16:1–8 is the earliest extant narrative generally identified; dating disputed | Matthew and Luke share Synoptic relationships; John independence debated | Gospel narratives | Not by itself | Attestation of empty-tomb narrative; objective historicity requires further evaluation |
| Romulus appeared after disappearance | Plutarch *Romulus* 28 reports Julius Proculus's oath | Proculus's original testimony does not survive | Plutarch, centuries after purported event | Yes, apotheosis | Evidence for the tradition, not for occurrence of appearance |
| Apollonius appeared after death | Philostratus *Life of Apollonius* 8.31 narrates youth's experience | Youth's original testimony not extant | Philostratus, much later | Yes, postmortem apparition | Evidence for the tradition; account includes a half-asleep experience |

## Findings and falsification conditions

1. Caesar's assassination has contemporary correspondence about its political aftermath, including correspondence involving conspirators. Its evidence differs in kind from the later literary testimony concerning Romulus and Apollonius.
2. The Christian resurrection case has early firsthand *experience-claim* evidence from Paul, which the two comparators here lack in extant form. This is a documentary advantage for the narrower proposition that Paul sincerely claimed an experience, but sincerity itself requires analysis.
3. The proposition 'Paul said he saw Jesus' cannot be assigned the same evidentiary score as 'Jesus bodily rose.' Likewise, 'Proculus was reported to say he saw Romulus' is distinct from 'Romulus appeared.'
4. The resurrection's proposed exceptional *joint evidentiary profile* remains an untested hypothesis, pending broader sampling, independence controls and source criticism.
5. A parity critique succeeds only if standards are shown to differ for *matched* propositions with relevantly similar background considerations. Differences in priors are analytically relevant, but must be made explicit and defended.
