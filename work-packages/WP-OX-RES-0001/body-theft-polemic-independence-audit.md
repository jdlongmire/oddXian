# Body-Theft Polemic: Independence and Historical Discrimination Audit

WP-OX-RES-0001 | 2026-10-08

## Purpose

Test whether the body-theft counterclaim can be traced independently of Matthew, and distinguish (a) an early dispute over a missing body, (b) the historicity of guards, and (c) the historicity of an empty tomb. Apply symmetric scrutiny to affirmative and skeptical reconstructions.

## Source inventory

1. **Matthew 27:62–66; 28:11–15**. Earliest extant detailed narrative of guards, bribery and the accusation that disciples stole the body. Matthew asserts the accusation circulated 'to this day'. This is a first-century Christian author's report of an opposing claim, not a surviving document written by an opponent.
2. **Justin Martyr, Dialogue with Trypho 108**, mid-second century. Justin explicitly alleges that Jewish representatives disseminated a claim that Jesus' disciples stole his body and falsely announced resurrection. Justin does not present an independently dated hostile text, name the alleged emissaries, or establish his source's independence from Matthew.
3. **Gospel of Peter 8–11**, usually placed in the second century (date and compositional layers disputed). Elaborates a guard and tomb narrative. Lack of verbal agreement alone cannot establish independence; literary and oral dependence on canonical passion traditions remain contested.
4. **Tertullian, Apology 21**, late second century. Repeats an apologetic narrative concerning guards and accusations; a later Christian witness to polemical persistence, not a first-century hostile deposition.
5. **Later Jewish polemical material**, including Toledot Yeshu traditions, cannot be projected back to the first century without a defensible transmission history.

## Scholarly reconstructions in dialogue

**Craig, 'The Guard at the Tomb'** argues the sequence of charge and countercharge suggests an earlier controversy and that a guard is not easily explained as an invented Christian answer. He nevertheless acknowledges significant objections and calls the guard's historicity an open question. His inference from the theft accusation to opponents' acknowledgment of an empty tomb depends on establishing the antiquity and authenticity of the adversarial tradition. Source: https://www.reasonablefaith.org/writings/scholarly-writings/historical-jesus/the-guard-at-the-tomb

**Kankaanniemi (2010), The Guards of the Tomb** proposes a pre-Matthean controversy but hypothesizes that opponents may originally have introduced guards to make the theft accusation plausible. This is an interesting alternative reconstruction, not a preserved original opponent statement. Source: https://www.doria.fi/handle/10024/67076

**Loke, Investigating the Resurrection of Jesus Christ, chapter 'What happened to Jesus’ physical body?'** argues that the guards story was early and publicly falsifiable, so fabrication would have damaged credibility. This is an argument from expected public contestation. It requires independently justified assumptions about audience access, the story's precise circulation date, and whether any failed verification would be preserved. Source: https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/oa-mono/10.4324/9781003037255-6/happened-jesus-physical-body-andrew-loke

## Competing transmission models

**Model A: Authentic early hostile theft counterclaim.** Opponents responded to an early Christian empty-tomb claim by alleging removal of the body; Matthew preserves a developed form; Justin transmits continued controversy. This explains why the polemic specifically concerns body removal, but the hostile original is not preserved.

**Model B: Early Christian apologetic inference.** Christian storytellers anticipated the natural explanation for a missing body, constructed a theft-and-guards narrative, and later writers repeated it. This explains the Christian-only surviving provenance and Matthean concentration, but must account for the allegation's later persistence.

**Model C: Mixed polemical development.** A real dispute over a tomb or corpse developed into increasingly specific theft, guards, sleep, and bribery claims, with uncertain attribution of each element. This avoids all-or-nothing treatment but must specify testable chronological and literary expectations.

These are explanatory candidates, not measured probabilities. The available evidence does not justify numeric likelihood ratios.

## Discriminating observations

- Justin's explicit repetition is evidence of a second-century Christian *report* of a hostile accusation. Without independent source tracing, it cannot be multiplied as a second independent hostile attestation.
- Matthew's allegation that a counterclaim was circulating in his own day is potentially important, especially if a pre-Matthean layer can be independently demonstrated. The mere phrase 'to this day' does not establish its origin date.
- The guards may be historical, apologetically developed, or partly traditional. The truth of a missing-body claim does not logically require guards; the guards' historicity does not establish resurrection.
- A theft explanation, if genuinely advanced by informed opponents about the actual tomb, would be relevant adversarial evidence that a missing body needed explanation. But that conditional cannot be collapsed into the assertion that opponents *conceded* an empty tomb.
- Paul's early appearance testimony and the 500 report remain separate evidence streams. Neither automatically independently confirms the location or condition of a burial site.

## Provisional verdict

**Pre-Matthean body-theft controversy:** plausible and argued by affirmative scholars, but not independently demonstrated from an extant hostile first-century document.

**Independence of Justin from Matthew:** not established. Justin strengthens evidence for later circulation of the accusation, not the independent source count for the earliest dispute.

**Guards historicity:** open; no source-critical shortcut.

**Empty tomb:** the theft controversy offers *conditional* adversarial support if the counterclaim is authentic and early. The claim must also be evaluated using Mark's tomb narrative, burial traditions, and historical contextual constraints.
