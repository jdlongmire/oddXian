# Greek ὤφθη, Formula Dating, and James: Focused Source-Critical Note v0.1

WP-OX-RES-0001 | 2026-10-08

## Findings

**1. Lexical question.** The aorist passive ὤφθη (ōphthē) in 1 Cor 15:5–8 is conventionally rendered 'appeared' or 'was seen'. Lexical meaning alone does not settle whether the reported appearance was bodily, visionary, or a divinely disclosed manifestation. Read the verb within Paul's argument about resurrection bodies in 1 Cor 15:35–54, the burial/resurrection formula in 15:3–4, and Jewish-Greek theophany usage. Do not infer from semantic openness that Paul believed Jesus was merely a disembodied spirit.

**Relevant published disagreement:** O'Collins (2014, pp. 128–143), abstract, argues appearance language suggests visual perception, albeit graced seeing; Gieniusz (2019, pp. 481–492), abstract, finds ὤφθη + dative poorly suited as standalone proof of factual appearances and stresses eschatological function. Ware (2014), abstract, argues common arguments on both sides are inadequate and investigates the formula's resurrection verb. The disagreement is substantive, not reducible to 'believer versus skeptic'. These claims are attributed to publicly accessible abstracts, not uninspected full texts.

**2. Formula extent and dating.** Paul explicitly says he received and transmitted the material (1 Cor 15:3). Verses 3b–5 are widely analyzed as an inherited formula; the boundaries of the inherited material are disputed, particularly whether 6a and 7 belong to it. Moffitt (2007/2008, pp. 49–73) addresses the extent of Paul's citation and discusses competing traditions associated with Peter and James. The text establishes a tradition earlier than the letter; precise year-of-origin claims require independently defended chronology, and dating verses 3–5 must not be conflated with dating the entire 3–8 list.

**3. Bodily meaning and empty tomb.** Paul contrasts a perishable body with a transformed, Spirit-animated resurrection body (1 Cor 15:42–49). A 'spiritual body' does not straightforwardly mean a non-body. A published New Testament Studies study, 'Resurrection in Paganism and the Question of an Empty Tomb in 1 Corinthians 15', argues that burial plus rising would conventionally imply an emptied grave in the relevant cultural setting. This is evidence for **conceptual presupposition**, not independent observation of an actual empty tomb. Ancient non-Christian resurrection analogues also complicate an unqualified claim that the *idea* was historically unprecedented.

**4. James.** Paul independently of the later Gospel narratives identifies James as a person he met (Gal 1:19) and reports an appearance to James (1 Cor 15:7). Josephus (Ant. 20.9.1) independently identifies James as Jesus' brother. John 7:5 reports Jesus' brothers did not believe in him during his ministry, but this is a later narrative source; it does not document James's inner state individually, the exact timing of a change, or the causal mechanism. The statement 'James converted because he saw the risen Jesus' remains a plausible explanatory reconstruction, not a separately documented first-person admission by James.

## Evidence-versus-inference table

| Claim | Support status |
|---|---|
| Paul reports his own appearance experience | Direct first-person epistolary attestation |
| Paul transmits appearance tradition concerning Cephas and James | Direct evidence of report, not separate extant depositions |
| Paul's resurrection concept is bodily/transformed rather than a ghost-only survival | Strong contextual reading of 1 Cor 15, with interpretive nuances |
| ὤφθη itself proves tactile physical contact | Not established by lexeme alone |
| Entire appearance list dates within months of crucifixion | Not established without additional chronology and formula-boundary arguments |
| James was once skeptical | Narrative evidence of brothers' unbelief, but James-specific reconstruction uncertain |
| James changed beliefs because of a verifiable bodily encounter | Causal historical inference; not directly independently attested |
| Early Christians could have presupposed an empty tomb | Arguable cultural-semantic inference; not a witnessed tomb inspection |

## Result for the inductive case

The earliest surviving Pauline witness is much stronger evidence against a **purely late-invention** model than against a **specified early-experience plus transmission** model. At the same time, Paul's transformed-bodily-resurrection framework challenges any alternative that equates his claim with belief in mere ghostly survival. Neither lexical analysis nor tradition dating alone yields a defensible Bayes factor for bodily resurrection.
